Climate-induced risks, adaptation, and mitigation responses: a comparative study on climate-stressed coastal communities

Why do neighboring coastal communities experience climate-related pressures differently—and which locally adopted responses help them adapt?

Sadhon Chandra Swarnokar · Sadia Islam Mou · Sutapa Dey Sharmi · Afif Iftikhar · Sabrina Jesmin

The investigation

Research question & objectives

The study moves beyond a single description of coastal climate vulnerability by comparing what residents of three unions experience and how local responses are supported or constrained.

How do perceived climate risks, related livelihood pressures and locally adopted adaptation strategies differ across Tildanga, Kamarkhola and Sutarkhali—and what limits their effectiveness and wider adoption?

Objective one

Compare climate pressures

Document community perceptions of cyclones, flooding, salinity, water scarcity and other climate-related events across the three unions.

Objective two

Examine local responses

Identify household and community adaptation practices and compare reported adoption, perceived effectiveness and practical limitations.

Objective three

Understand institutional barriers

Assess the roles of government, NGOs and local participation in sustaining water security, livelihoods, infrastructure and community resilience.

Where the study took place

Three neighboring coastal unions

Tildanga, Kamarkhola and Sutarkhali are in Dacope Upazila, southwestern Bangladesh, near the Sundarbans. They share exposure to coastal hazards but differ in livelihood patterns and reported local pressures. The sites were purposefully selected because of their climate-related vulnerabilities and adaptation practices.

How the evidence was collected

Mixed-methods research design

Quantitative survey responses were combined with focus groups and interviews, allowing the authors to compare perceived climate hazards with accounts of everyday adaptation, governance and support gaps.

Community survey

150 structured questionnaires

Fifty respondents per union described household backgrounds, climate risk perceptions, livelihoods and adaptive practices. Perceived event frequency was assessed using a five-point scale.

Qualitative fieldwork

6 FGDs and 30 interviews

Six focus groups, 15 key-informant interviews and 15 in-depth interviews explored local strategies, obstacles and institutional contexts.

Analysis

Comparison & thematic interpretation

Survey information was summarized descriptively, including percentages and perception scores; qualitative accounts were examined thematically.

How to read these results: The charts summarize respondents’ experiences and ratings. They are not long-term instrument measurements of cyclone intensity, soil salinity or other physical hazards.

Key finding · climate hazards

The three unions reported different risk profiles

Kamarkhola respondents reported particularly frequent cyclones and flooding, while salinity was a prominent issue in Tildanga and Sutarkhali. These distinctions matter because the same intervention may not respond equally well to each union’s needs.

Key finding · livelihoods & resources

Water stress and livelihood pressures were connected

Respondents reported declining soil fertility, reduced agricultural production, freshwater shortages and stresses on fisheries and forest products. The impact of these overlapping pressures varied among the three unions.

86–96%Reported declining soil fertility across the three unions
44–92%Reported reductions in agricultural output
54–88%Reported a reduction in fish-species diversity

The percentages above represent surveyed perceptions of environmental and livelihood change, not independently validated biological or agricultural measurements.

Key finding · community responses

Communities were adapting, but the options were uneven

Reported responses included rainwater harvesting, salt-tolerant cultivation, elevated housing, early warning systems, social forestry and alternative livelihood training. Some measures were more widely adopted than others, and many faced limitations in financing, infrastructure or maintenance.

How the study framed the relationships

Its conceptual framework links climate hazards to water access, agriculture, infrastructure and migration, alongside potential community and institutional responses. It illustrates proposed pathways rather than measuring causal effects.

Key finding · barriers & institutional support

Local action was constrained by the capacity to sustain it

Communities had developed practical responses to risk, but inadequate finance, technical support, training, infrastructure maintenance and coordination limited their continuation and expansion.

Resources

Financing & maintenance

Households reported barriers to extending rainwater systems, resilient infrastructure and livelihood alternatives beyond initial investments.

Technical capacity

Training & information

New agricultural practices, service delivery and preparedness depend on continuing technical guidance and locally accessible knowledge.

Coordination

Institutions & participation

Community groups, local government and NGOs need more consistent coordination and inclusive involvement to sustain locally appropriate responses.

What the evidence means

Interpretation, contributions & limitations

The study shows that coastal resilience is not only a question of exposure to hazards. It also depends on which livelihood and water-security pressures are experienced locally, and whether communities have the resources and institutional support to respond.

Finding

Different places need different responses

Hazard and livelihood perceptions varied among the neighboring unions; a uniform strategy would overlook some of those differences.

Practical relevance

Combine local knowledge with support

Community-led measures require reliable financing, maintenance, knowledge-sharing and responsive institutions to remain useful.

Evidence boundary

Report perceptions as perceptions

The purposively selected study locations and self-reported cross-sectional information do not establish causal impacts or represent every coastal community.

Research contribution: The article brings together survey-based comparisons and qualitative accounts of adaptation, local governance and everyday livelihood pressures, showing why context-specific planning matters across three coastal unions.
