Residential greenness and perinatal depression in low- and middle-income countries: A multi-country population-based study

How does satellite-measured vegetation surrounding women’s homes relate to depressive symptoms during pregnancy and the year after childbirth?

Why this research matters

Research question & objectives

Perinatal mental health is shaped by social, reproductive, and environmental conditions. This study investigated residential greenness alongside ambient temperature across three low- and middle-income countries.

Is higher residential greenness associated with lower odds of perinatal depressive symptoms, after accounting for measured individual and environmental factors?

Objective 1

Estimate the main association

Link satellite-derived vegetation measures to depression screening outcomes in a pooled, three-country sample.

Objective 2

Examine population differences

Evaluate whether the estimated association varies across countries and sociodemographic groups.

Objective 3

Assess environmental context

Examine ambient temperature, potential greenness–temperature interactions and sensitivity to alternative specifications.

Study population

Three countries, one harmonized analysis

The analysis used Demographic and Health Surveys conducted during 2022–2023. Eligible participants were pregnant or had given birth in the preceding 12 months and had the required depression-screening and exposure data.

3,145Bangladesh · 2022 survey
2,906Mozambique · 2022–2023 survey
838Nepal · 2022 survey

Outcome definition: depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), with a score of 5 or more indicating at least mild symptoms. The measure is a screening outcome, not a confirmed clinical diagnosis of depression.

How the study was conducted

From satellite observations to adjusted statistical associations

Environmental exposures were associated with anonymized DHS sampling clusters rather than individual residential addresses. Mixed-effects models accounted for clustering, survey weighting and measured confounding variables.

01 · Exposure

Measure residential vegetation

Annual greenness was derived from 30 m Landsat 8 surface reflectance using NDVI, with EVI as a sensitivity measure. Google Earth Engine supported exposure processing.

02 · Spatial linkage

Link exposure to survey clusters

Mean environmental exposure was extracted within 2 km urban and 5 km rural buffers to account for DHS geographic displacement. Ambient temperature came from ERA5-Land.

03 · Statistical analysis

Estimate adjusted odds ratios

Survey-weighted, cluster-level mixed-effects logistic models adjusted for country, age, residence, education, wealth, marital status, reproductive factors and temperature.

Interpretation: NDVI indicates vegetation density around survey locations. It does not tell us whether women could enter green spaces, what types of vegetation were present, or how much time they spent outdoors.

Primary result

Greater residential greenness was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms

The inverse association remained after adjustment for demographic, reproductive, and temperature variables. The statistical estimate describes an association, not a change in an individual person’s probability or proof of protection.

Fully adjusted associationOR 0.9195% CI 0.842–0.977 · per one SD higher NDVI

Equivalent to approximately 9% lower adjusted odds of screening positive for depressive symptoms—not 9% lower risk, and not proof that increasing greenness would prevent depression.

Subgroup analysis

The pooled association was not uniform across populations

The stratified analysis found a statistically detectable inverse association in Mozambique, among younger women, rural residents, women with primary education and those in the richer wealth group. Estimates in other categories were more uncertain.

0.71NDVI odds ratio in Mozambique · 95% CI 0.593–0.845
0.85NDVI odds ratio among rural women · 95% CI 0.763–0.945
0.76NDVI odds ratio among women with primary education · 95% CI 0.669–0.864

Important: an association reaching statistical significance in one subgroup but not another does not, by itself, demonstrate that the effect differs between groups. Subgroup estimates should be interpreted alongside confidence intervals, sample sizes and context.

Temperature and interaction analyses

Warmer environments were associated with higher odds—but interaction was not confirmed

A one-SD increase in ambient temperature was associated with higher adjusted odds of depressive symptoms. Patterns in the stratified analyses suggested, but did not statistically establish, that greenness modified the temperature association.

1.44Fully adjusted temperature OR · 95% CI 1.290–1.625
0.474p-value for NDVI × temperature-quartile interaction
0.090p-value for temperature × NDVI-quartile interaction

What this does—and does not—show: the temperature estimate represents higher adjusted odds, not a demonstrated causal effect. Neither formal interaction test reached conventional statistical significance, so the apparent buffering pattern should be treated as exploratory.

Sensitivity checks

Alternative vegetation index

Replacing NDVI with EVI produced a broadly consistent inverse association in the fully adjusted model (OR 0.88; 95% CI 0.815–0.945).

Additional air-pollution adjustment

The association remained inverse after adding PM2.5 to a sensitivity model (OR 0.88; 95% CI 0.817–0.952).

Scientific interpretation

What the combined evidence tells us

This population-based study found an inverse association between surrounding vegetation and depressive symptoms, alongside a positive association with ambient temperature. The evidence contributes to research on environmental conditions and maternal mental health, without establishing causal mechanisms.

Meaningful pooled association

The adjusted NDVI association persisted across multiple model specifications and alternative exposure measures.

Country and social context matter

Subgroup analyses suggest variation, but they do not explain the underlying reasons or prove different causal effects.

Planning relevance, with caution

Residential vegetation may matter for population health, but access, vegetation type, and individual exposure require further study.

Limitations of the evidence

  • Cross-sectional data: associations cannot determine cause and effect or establish whether greenness preceded symptoms in an individual.
  • Exposure resolution: anonymized survey clusters and 2/5 km buffers cannot capture true personal exposure or daily mobility.
  • Greenness versus use: NDVI cannot distinguish parks from fields or evaluate access, quality and actual contact with nature.
  • Outcome and uncertainty: PHQ-9 screens symptoms; unmeasured social or environmental factors may still influence the association. Interaction evidence was exploratory, not statistically confirmed.
