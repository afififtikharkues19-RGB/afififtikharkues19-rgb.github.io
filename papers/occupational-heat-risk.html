Occupational heat risk perceptions and behavioral adaptation strategies among construction and welding workers in Bangladesh

How do construction and welding workers experience workplace heat, and what shapes the measures they take to protect themselves?

Research focus

Research question and objectives

Extreme heat affects workers performing physical labor outdoors and near equipment that produces heat. Awareness does not always translate into protection.

How do construction and welding workers perceive heat risks, what symptoms do they report, and which factors are associated with changes in protective behavior?

Objective 1

Understand perceived risk

Document climate awareness, workplace concerns, and physical and emotional experiences.

Objective 2

Compare adaptation

Examine water consumption, rest breaks, clothing, fans, work schedules and safety training.

Objective 3

Explain variation

Assess how occupation, experience and risk perception relate to the direction of behavior changes.

Study design

A field survey during the hot season

The cross-sectional survey took place in Dhaka, Khulna and Barishal between 2 May and 5 July 2024. Purposive snowball recruitment was used because no complete register of these workers was available.

01 · Data

Structured questionnaire

Thirty-seven questions covered work settings, climate awareness, symptoms, protection and ten adaptation practices.

02 · Comparisons

Descriptive findings

Responses were compared between 185 construction and 135 welding workers; experiences were self-reported.

03 · Models

Multinomial regression

Each behavior was analyzed as increasing, decreasing or remaining unchanged; adjusted regression analyses used the 317 male respondents.

Evidence boundary: The research did not measure each worker’s actual heat dose, diagnose clinical illness or establish causal effects.

Workplace conditions

Different work environments and protection

Construction workers were generally more exposed to outdoor conditions, whereas welding workers more often reported longer workdays and less access to heat-safety information.

Self-reported experiences

Physical symptoms and emotional strain

Workers commonly reported heat-associated sweating, thirst, cramps, fatigue and headaches, alongside irritability and difficulty controlling temper. Emotional responses were not validated clinical diagnoses.

70.7%Welding workers reporting excessive sweating “always” (construction: 51.3%)
57.8%Welding workers reporting frequent thirst “always” (construction: 45.5%)
27.0%Welding workers reporting muscle cramps “always” (construction: 14.3%)

Behavioral responses

Protective practices increased unevenly

Many respondents said they used more water and breaks during hot weather. Other options, particularly safety training and equipment, were less consistently practiced.

80%Increased drinking cool water before feeling thirsty
69%Increased taking breaks in shaded or cooler places
58%Increased loose or light-colored clothing use

Practical point: People cannot adapt through individual choices alone if protected breaks, cooling and workplace training are not available.

Statistical findings

Occupation and experience were associated with different changes

Multinomial logistic regressions compared the odds of reporting an increase or decrease in each specific behavior against reporting no change. Confidence intervals were sometimes wide.

Scientific interpretation

Heat protection requires more than risk awareness

Most workers recognized heat risks and adopted some personal responses, yet conditions and safety support differed considerably between the two work settings.

Risk awareness was widespread

Participants associated strenuous labor, hours, sunlight and hot air with heat stress.

Adaptation depended on context

Changes in breaks, clothing, cooling and work schedules differed across occupations.

Workplace support matters

Training, warnings, shade and reasonable workloads require institutional protections.

Limitations of the evidence

  • Selection: purposive snowball sampling from three cities limits generalization.
  • Measurement: symptoms were self-reported; personal thermal exposure and clinical outcomes were not measured.
  • Model population: 320 surveyed, but regression analyses included 317 men because only three female respondents were available.
  • Inference: the cross-sectional design cannot establish causation, and some model confidence intervals were wide.
