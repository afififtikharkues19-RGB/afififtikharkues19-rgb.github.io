Spatially Offset Curvature–Migration Associations in a Tidal River Corridor

Multi-Decadal Evidence from the Rupsha–Pasur

Afif Iftikhar & Sadhon Chandra Swarnokar · Khulna University

Does river curvature measured upstream help explain where a tide-influenced channel subsequently migrates? This study uses Landsat-derived geometry and hierarchical Bayesian modeling to evaluate that relationship, its reach-to-reach variation, and the limits of the satellite evidence.

Study area & observed channel change

The analysis examines approximately 121.6 km of the tide-influenced Rupsha–Pasur river corridor in southwestern Bangladesh. Its spatial domain was established from a fixed reference axis; the final statistical dataset uses 1,014 stations meeting the common-support requirements across all six curvature offsets.

Channel position was reconstructed from satellite-observed outer water–land boundary proxies, rather than field-surveyed geomorphic banks. The multi-temporal geometry reveals uneven mapped-centerline displacement along the corridor, illustrated by the examples and downstream profile in Figure 2.

Data & methodology

Seventy primary Landsat Collection 2 surface-reflectance images supported 35 analysis years spanning 1988–2025. Water was identified using MNDWI and row-specific Otsu thresholds; topology-aware processing then separated the main river from connected surrounding water and generated annual boundary proxies, center positions and widths.

How upstream curvature was tested

Start-state signed, width-normalized curvature (κB) was evaluated at 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 channel widths (B) upstream of each migration station. These are spatial offsets, not time lags. Signed migration was measured in a downstream-referenced coordinate system; opposite curvature and migration signs correspond to outward-aligned movement under that convention.

The primary inferential model was a hierarchical Student-t regression fitted in PyMC to 25,923 station-period observations across 34 migration periods and 12 spatial blocks. A separate 2,000-realization geometry ensemble assessed the sensitivity of mapped curvature and migration; it was not pooled into, or used to adjust, the primary Bayesian posterior.

Results

Observed migration patterns

Mapped migration varied across periods and reaches. Of the 25,923 observations, 56.1% had exactly zero extracted centerline displacement; these mapped zeros do not establish that the physical channel was immobile. The response also contained substantial nonzero changes and a long-tailed magnitude distribution.

Global curvature–migration association

Across the six tested positions, only the 2.0B upstream coefficient had a corridor-wide 95% credible interval entirely below zero. Under the adopted sign convention, this is a small outward-aligned association between antecedent curvature and later mapped-centerline migration.

Interpretation. The 2.0B result was the clearest of the six tested geometric relationships; it is not a demonstrated hydraulic lag, a universal migration distance, or evidence that curvature is a strong predictor of channel change. The standardized mean is about 0.59% of the migration-response standard deviation.

Spatial heterogeneity across reaches

The global mean conceals meaningful variation: at 2.0B, 10 of 12 block posterior means were negative, but none of the individual block-level 95% credible intervals was entirely negative. Only four of 72 block-by-offset intervals excluded zero. One of those, B07 at 1.5B, had a contrasting inward-aligned estimate; the resolved estimates in B09 and B10 were outward-aligned.

These patterns indicate reach-dependent geometric associations rather than a uniform, corridor-wide migration response.

Uncertainty & model evaluation

Measurement uncertainty is central to the interpretation of this study. The estimated error thresholds reflect satellite boundary delineation, water-level variation and positional uncertainty, while statistical diagnostics reveal additional limits.

Mapped displacement

Empirical P95 centerline-error thresholds were 50.79, 49.42 and 53.21 m for 1-, 2- and 3-year gaps. Only 191 of 25,923 individual mapped displacements exceeded their respective thresholds; the others cannot be treated as confirmed immobility.

Geometry sensitivity

The 2,000-realization joint geometry ensemble recomputed signed migration and curvature, but held channel width fixed and was not incorporated into the Bayesian likelihood. The reported credible intervals are not measurement-error adjusted.

Statistical limitations

The primary posterior retained two divergent transitions out of 8,000 draws. Residual spatial dependence remained, and the continuous Student-t model did not reproduce the large concentration of exact-zero mapped displacements.

A separate hierarchical Bernoulli analysis described the occurrence of recorded nonzero mapped displacement. It did not estimate signed migration direction, establish physical bank stability, or replace the primary continuous model.

Conclusion

The study identifies a small, spatially offset and reach-dependent association between antecedent signed curvature and subsequent mapped-centerline migration in the tidal Rupsha–Pasur corridor. The strongest corridor-wide evidence among the evaluated offsets was found at 2.0B, while local estimates varied and remained uncertain. The findings are observational and corridor-specific; they do not establish a universal physical mechanism or response distance.
