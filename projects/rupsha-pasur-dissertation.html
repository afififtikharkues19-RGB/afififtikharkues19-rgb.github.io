River change, land use & livelihoods along the Rupsha–Pasur

How did the river landscape change over 30 years, which land uses changed alongside it, and what did households living near the corridor report about vulnerability and adaptation?

The investigation

Research question & objectives

The research addresses a gap between three kinds of evidence that are often examined separately: river-boundary movement, changes to surrounding land cover, and household livelihood conditions.

Where did the Rupsha–Pasur river corridor change between 1994 and 2024, how did land cover change in the same landscape, and what patterns of vulnerability and adaptation did nearby households report?

Objective one

Map environmental change

Quantify changing river width, area, mapped erosion and accretion; classify land cover through time; and compare land-use transitions near river-active areas.

Objective two

Assess livelihood conditions

Describe household vulnerability and adaptation, and analyze how selected social, economic and river-exposure characteristics are associated with income and adoption of adaptations.

Satellite layer

River boundaries

How the mapped channel moved and changed size.

Landscape layer

Land cover

Which classes persisted or changed within the corridor.

Household layer

Livelihoods

What households reported about land, vulnerability and adaptation.

How to read the connections: these are complementary spatial and survey observations. The study documents co-occurrence and statistical associations; it does not demonstrate that river change caused a particular land conversion or household outcome.

Where the study took place

The tidal Rupsha–Pasur landscape

The corridor stretches from the Khulna urban reach southward toward Mongla and the northern Sundarbans. Its setting includes tidal waterways, agriculture, aquaculture, settlements and mangrove-influenced landscapes.

How the study was carried out

Data & analytical approach

The work combined a thirty-year satellite reconstruction with spatial overlay analysis and primary household interviews. Each evidence layer had its own scale, method and uncertainty.

River geometry

Mapping channel change

Landsat Collection 2 imagery for 1994, 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, 2019 and 2024; MNDWI water-boundary extraction; visual correction; approximately 246 cross-channel transects; measurements of mapped width, water-surface area and bank-side displacement; polygon overlays for erosion and accretion.

Land-cover analysis

Tracking transitions

Supervised maximum-likelihood classification into vegetation, cropland, waterbody, barren land and urban classes. A fixed 1,800 km² domain provided corridor-wide trends; a separate 1-km river-active belt and mapped erosion, accretion and stable zones supported spatial comparison.

Household evidence

Measuring vulnerability

Survey of 200 households in Rupsha, Batiaghata and Dacope. Descriptive statistics, composite Livelihood Vulnerability Index (LVI), LVI-IPCC components, multiple regression of household income, logistic regression of adaptation adoption, and reported land-use histories.

Important measurement distinction: satellite-extracted water–land boundaries are observations at each image's water level—not surveyed geomorphic banks. The seven snapshots were not normalized to a common tidal stage.

Results · River morphology

The mapped channel became wider

Both mean mapped width and mapped channel-water area increased over the study period. These measurements describe change in the observed channel geometry, not a direct measurement of sediment transport or tidal hydraulics.

486.4 → 581.3 mMean mapped channel width, 1994 to 2024
62.84 → 75.46 km²Mapped channel-water area, 1994 to 2024
+12.62 km²Net change in mapped channel-water area

Erosion and accretion, taken together

Across six five-year periods, mapped erosion exceeded the numerically reconciled accretion total. The difference equals the endpoint increase in channel-water area.

23.44 km²Closure-reconciled accretion

Where the mapped boundary changed

The spatial overlay highlights erosion and accretion along different portions of the corridor. The map and reconciled numerical totals answer different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable.

Interpreting the apparent rate increase: mapped erosion was 0.97 km²/year in 1994–1999 and 1.59 km²/year in 2019–2024, but the interval pattern was not monotonic and no formal acceleration test was conducted.

Results · Land-use transitions

The surrounding landscape changed too

Across the fixed 1,800 km² classification domain, cropland and vegetation declined, while mapped water surfaces and built-up areas increased. The pattern is not reducible to river widening alone.

18.5% → 9.2%Cropland share (−9.3 percentage points)
10.2% → 20.1%Waterbody share (+9.9 points)
0.8% → 8.2%Urban share (+7.4 points)

What changed close to the river?

A separate pixel-wise comparison inside a 1-km belt around the mapped bankline-shift envelope identified the largest land-cover exchanges near the river. This belt is a different area from the 1,800 km² corridor-wide domain.

78.50 km²

Cropland → waterbody

The largest mapped class transition inside the river-active belt. Waterbody combines channels, ponds, canals and aquaculture surfaces.

39.75 km²

Cropland → urban

The second-largest mapped transition in the river-active belt, indicating substantial built-up expansion in the comparison area.

In the erosion-zone composition comparison, 44.7% of the mapped erosion area had been classified as cropland in the baseline land-cover map. This describes spatial overlap, not a causal estimate of river-induced agricultural loss.

What 'waterbody' does not tell us: this satellite class cannot separate aquaculture ponds from natural water and river channels. The 178.2 km² increase in corridor-wide waterbody is much larger than the 12.62 km² net channel-water-area increase, so the two should never be presented as equivalent.

Results · Household conditions

How households described land & livelihoods

The 200-household survey adds lived, household-scale information to the satellite patterns. It does not validate the satellite classification, and older land-use histories depend on participant recall.

59.5%reported persistence of their broad land-use class
33households reported cropland-to-aquaculture conversion
64.0%reported adopting at least one adaptation measure

Livelihood vulnerability is multidimensional

The overall composite LVI was 0.443. The separate LVI-IPCC contributing-factor score was −0.054; the two values use different constructions and must not be compared as if they were the same scale. Food and water sensitivity were prominent components.

Descriptive composite LVI by surveyed area: Dacope 0.475; Batiaghata 0.427; Rupsha 0.410. The survey allocation does not support treating these as population-representative rankings.

Adapting to environmental and livelihood pressures

128 households reported at least one adaptation measure, and adopters reported using an average of 3.5 strategies. Water-related measures, diversified income and financial coping were commonly reported.

Reported obstacles

Money and information

Among 72 non-adopters, 38 named lack of money and 20 named lack of information as their main barrier.

Statistical associations

Training and group membership

In the logistic model, training (odds ratio 3.08) and NGO/cooperative membership (2.19) were associated with higher odds of adaptation adoption. These associations do not show that either factor caused adoption.

Synthesis

What the combined evidence tells us

The value of this dissertation lies in putting three distinct observation systems alongside one another—without mistaking correlation or spatial proximity for explanation.

Physical change

The measured water-boundary geometry widened and changed across the multi-decadal record, and erosion and accretion varied by reach and period.

Landscape transformation

Cropland fell while mapped waterbody and urban classes rose; some of the largest land-cover exchanges occurred near river-active areas.

Household realities

Survey responses revealed livelihood differences, self-reported land conversions, uneven vulnerability components and diverse adaptation practices.

What the study can—and cannot—establish

Supported by the evidence

Documented patterns

  • Mapped water-boundary and channel-area changes at seven dates.
  • Land-cover composition and transitions in explicitly defined domains.
  • Survey-reported household characteristics, vulnerability, land-use history and adaptation.
  • Statistical associations within the cross-sectional household analysis.
Not established

Causal or fully resolved mechanisms

  • Tide-normalized geomorphic-bank migration or measured sediment-transport mechanisms.
  • A causal effect of river change on specific land-use or livelihood outcomes.
  • A distinct satellite-derived aquaculture class or independent validation for 1994/1999 land-cover maps.
  • Population-representative vulnerability rankings or tested effectiveness of adaptation strategies.

Principal limitations: 30 m imagery, no tidal-stage normalization, incomplete retained threshold/GIS processing records, 76.7–79.8% classification accuracy in the validated 2004–2024 years, a fixed 1-km belt without width-sensitivity analysis, and a cross-sectional household survey with retrospective recall. The channel-area and erosion–accretion closure audit improves numerical consistency but does not remove mapping uncertainty.

Research significance

From identifying change to understanding its context

The dissertation provides a connected account of changing tidal river boundaries, adjacent land-cover transitions and household vulnerability along the Rupsha–Pasur corridor. It supports better-targeted monitoring and future investigation, particularly research that can resolve tidal-stage effects, distinguish aquaculture from other water surfaces, and test physical explanations against observations.
