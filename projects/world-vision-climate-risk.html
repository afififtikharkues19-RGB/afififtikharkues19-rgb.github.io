Climate Risk, Impacts & Vulnerability in Coastal Bangladesh

How do climate-related hazards, household vulnerability and preparedness differ between Paikgachha and Sarankhola—and what kinds of adaptation responses should be prioritized?

A World Vision-supported mixed-methods assessment integrating household evidence, geospatial vulnerability mapping and planning tools across two southwestern coastal upazilas.

Research question

Understanding risk beyond the hazard itself

Where do exposure, household sensitivity and limits to adaptation combine to create heightened climate vulnerability, and how can that evidence guide localized resilience planning?

The study considered impacts on livelihoods, water, food security, health and education while examining community preparedness and existing adaptation practices.

01

Assess impacts

Support decisions

Study area & evidence base

Two coastal communities, different risk conditions

Paikgachha and Sarankhola experience interacting pressures from cyclones, floods, salinity intrusion, water insecurity and climate-sensitive livelihoods. The study compared how these pressures affect households and preparedness in two different coastal settings.

Data and methods

How the assessment was conducted

01 · Community evidence

Surveys and qualitative inquiry

Structured household questionnaires, FGDs, KIIs and case studies examined reported impacts, livelihoods, disaster preparedness and coping strategies.

02 · Spatial analysis

Geospatial vulnerability assessment

Household and spatial evidence were integrated to assess community exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity and to map variation in vulnerability.

03 · Comparative analysis

Trends and community capacity

Historical and perceived climate information, community vulnerability indicators and SWOT analysis informed comparisons between areas.

04 · Planning framework

Prioritization and monitoring

Multi-criteria analysis, scenario-based cost–benefit analysis, stakeholder mapping and a monitoring, evaluation and learning framework supported proposed responses.

How to read the results:

Household responses describe reported experiences and perceptions. Vulnerability classifications are derived assessments. Adaptation priorities and cost–benefit projections are planning outputs, not demonstrated future outcomes.

Key findings

What the assessment found

01 · Warnings and preparedness

Having information is not the same as acting on it

In Paikgachha, 69% of surveyed households reported receiving and acting on early warnings, compared with 48% in Sarankhola. Other respondents received warnings but did not consistently act, emphasizing that preparedness depends on more than message delivery.

02 · Livelihoods and mobility

Economic disruption does not produce the same response everywhere

Both areas reported pressures on agriculture, income and food availability. In the study's migration accounts, 75% of Paikgachha households reported one to three household members migrating over the previous decade; in Sarankhola, 79% reported no member migration. These are different response categories, so the figures describe contrasting patterns rather than directly comparable migration rates.

03 · Water and health

Safe-water disruptions intersect with reported health burdens

Households described recurring difficulty accessing safe drinking water, alongside climate-related health concerns. Waterborne diseases made up 40.05% of the health issues reported in Paikgachha and 45.48% in Sarankhola in the study summary. These percentages describe the report's categories of reported health concerns—not clinically verified disease incidence.

Spatial vulnerability

Where vulnerabilities concentrate

The assessment combined community indicators and spatial interpretation to identify within-upazila differences in vulnerability. These categories describe conditions assessed in the project; they are not forecasts of when or where future disasters will occur.

Interpretive caution:

The vulnerability classes depend on indicator choices, normalization, spatial inputs and judgment. The maps should be interpreted as spatial assessments rather than a single precise ranking of vulnerability across both upazilas.

From assessment to planning

Translating evidence into adaptation priorities

The report examined practical responses in water security, disaster preparedness, livelihoods, resilient infrastructure and local governance. It also proposed ways to rank, finance and monitor interventions.

Water & livelihoods

Strengthen everyday resilience

Increase safe-water storage and rainwater harvesting, and support diversification through climate-resilient farming and livelihood training.

Risk communication

Improve preparedness

Address warning-response gaps through more inclusive alerts, shelters, local preparedness plans and community drills.

Governance & learning

Track implementation

Strengthen institutional coordination, community participation and indicators for monitoring adaptation outcomes.

Professional research contribution

My role in the assessment

Geospatial research support · Khulna University research team

My contribution involved geospatial data management, organizing and integrating geographic layers with study information, spatial quality checks, and support for vulnerability mapping and project documentation. The household survey, community analysis and adaptation recommendations were produced through the wider collaborative team.

What these findings establish

  • Reported household experiences differ across the two sites.
  • Climate-related stressors affect several connected aspects of well-being.
  • Spatial assessment helps identify areas for further attention and local planning.

What they do not establish

  • Household perceptions are not direct proof of hazard trends.
  • Spatial vulnerability scores are not precise predictions.
  • Projected intervention benefits do not demonstrate achieved impact.
